\abstract{
\textbf{Purpose:} To characterize a pre-provisioned, PSK-authenticated application-layer exchange combining X25519 and ML-KEM-768 on an ESP32.
\textbf{Methods:} We document a FreeRTOS client, Python HTTP server, native C KEM and encrypted telemetry lifecycle. We reanalyse 100 archived ESP32-D0WD-V3 records at 160~MHz, keeping primitive, TLS and heap measurements as separate acquisitions.
\textbf{Results:} The 100 records marked HMAC-verified completion have mean instrumented latency \BenchmarkMeanMs~ms (SD \BenchmarkSdMs~ms; 95\% CI \BenchmarkLowMs~to~\BenchmarkHighMs~ms) and mean elapsed CCOUNT of \BenchmarkCyclesM~million cycles; the attempted-run denominator is unavailable. A separate primitive trace gives 592.80~ms for the summed X25519 operation means and 32.47~ms for the client ML-KEM-768 operations. These are not matched before-and-after or end-to-end comparisons. A separate, incomplete heap profile reports 13.8~KB and is not a campaign result.
\textbf{Conclusion:} The contribution is a source-available testbed and bounded implementation characterization. It is not a new primitive or combiner, a first-deployment claim, or evidence of advantage over TLS. Shared bidirectional traffic keys, incomplete historical binary provenance and unevaluated side channels constrain security conclusions.
}

\section{Introduction and research scope}
Embedded post-quantum experiments need more than primitive timing: readers must be able to inspect the network messages, authentication assumptions, task configuration and measurement boundaries. The released software~\cite{software} exposes these elements for a pre-provisioned, PSK-authenticated application-layer exchange on an ESP32-D0WD-V3 at 160~MHz. It combines X25519~\cite{b5,b19} and ML-KEM-768~\cite{b2} in a FreeRTOS client and asynchronous Python HTTP service. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} separates the implemented protocol path from the independently acquired evidence. The paper's contribution is this inspectable deployment and its bounded characterization, not a new KEM or a claim that PQC first became feasible on ESP32.

X-Wing already combines X25519 and ML-KEM-768 using a specified combiner and security analysis~\cite{b41}. Earlier work evaluated post-quantum TLS on embedded systems~\cite{b11}; recent studies examine post-quantum IoT authentication over TLS, hybrid ML-KEM-768/X25519 MQTT, hybrid TLS/OPC UA deployments, and TLS~1.3 PQC benchmarks that include ESP32~\cite{b44,b45,b46,b47}. These are close precedents, so the present paper does not claim algorithmic novelty, first use of hybrid PQC on ESP32, or general superiority. Its distinct scope is a certificate-free, pre-provisioned-PSK HTTP exchange with an encrypted telemetry lifecycle, documented as an inspectable implementation and evaluated through clearly separated acquisitions. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} compares the settings and limits of inference; it is a purposive scope comparison, not a systematic review or a harmonized quantitative benchmark. Neither X-Wing's proof nor TLS security properties transfer automatically to this construction.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Post-quantum transition and standardized KEMs}
The prospect of quantum algorithms affecting public-key cryptography has motivated migration planning as well as new cryptographic constructions~\cite{b1,b10,b27,b39}. NIST's standardization work led to ML-KEM, standardized in FIPS~203; the associated recommendations describe KEM use and key-establishment practice~\cite{b2,b9,b15}. ML-KEM is based on the module-lattice line of work developed from learning-with-errors constructions~\cite{b3,b4,b26,b28}. The Fujisaki-Okamoto transform is relevant background to CCA-secure KEM design, but the security claims in this paper remain limited to the standardized ML-KEM component and the properties actually modeled~\cite{b23}. These sources establish the cryptographic and migration context, but they do not imply that an application using a standardized KEM inherits a protocol-level security proof. This study therefore evaluates the documented integration and its evidence boundaries.

\subsection{Hybrid key establishment and protocol standards}
Hybrid designs combine classical and post-quantum key-establishment material so that security can be retained under stated assumptions about at least one component; their security depends on the combiner, transcript binding and protocol context~\cite{b8,b31,b32,b41}. The IETF has since published a general TLS~1.3 hybrid key-exchange construction and concrete X25519MLKEM768 groups~\cite{b17,b16}, alongside the TLS~1.3 protocol specification~\cite{b21}. Those standardized mechanisms have a different wire format, negotiation process and authentication context from the application-layer exchange studied here. In particular, this paper concatenates component outputs and applies its documented HKDF profile; it does not claim conformance to X-Wing or the TLS hybrid groups. The KEM and key-derivation interfaces are discussed in their own standards and guidance~\cite{b9,b20,b33}.

\subsection{Embedded implementations and post-quantum TLS}
Prior implementation work has optimized lattice-based cryptography for constrained processors, including Cortex-M4 implementations and arithmetic techniques for low-end 32-bit IoT devices~\cite{b6,b7,b12,b13}. Other work has integrated post-quantum algorithms into embedded TLS stacks and examined TLS authentication or handshake costs~\cite{b11,b14,b34,b38}. These studies provide important implementation and protocol baselines; their platforms, algorithm versions, authentication paths, libraries and measurement methods differ, so their numerical results are not pooled with the present campaign.

Recent IoT studies have evaluated post-quantum authentication and hybrid communications in TLS, MQTT and OPC UA settings~\cite{b44,b45,b46}. Especially close in platform and algorithm choice, Gillot et al. report a TLS~1.3 benchmark matrix that includes ESP32 and X25519/ML-KEM combinations~\cite{b47}. Their preprint makes clear that implementation and architecture can materially affect relative costs. It also means this manuscript cannot claim that it is the first ESP32 evaluation of X25519 plus ML-KEM or that a custom exchange is generally more efficient than PQ-TLS. The present work has a narrower purpose: to expose a pre-provisioned-PSK HTTP exchange, its telemetry lifecycle and its source-level measurement boundaries, then report archived observations without treating them as a matched comparison to TLS or the recent benchmark matrix.

\subsection{Implementation security and evaluation boundaries}
Authenticated encryption, message authentication and key derivation each have established interfaces and guidance~\cite{b22,b24,b29,b35,b36,b37}. Formal protocol analysis can test specified properties of an abstract model, while implementation-level leakage requires separate analysis; published lattice-cryptography side-channel studies illustrate why source-level intent alone cannot establish resistance~\cite{b18,b40}. We use ProVerif for the limited properties described in Section~\ref{sec:results}, and do not infer protection against later PSK compromise, concrete nonce misuse, device memory exposure or physical side channels. This distinction places the work alongside prior cryptographic and embedded evaluations without claiming those properties were experimentally demonstrated here.

\subsection{Research questions and study design}
The study asks three questions: which client operations dominate the cost of the selected hybrid implementation; what latency distribution and elapsed-cycle behavior occur in the archived application campaign; and how the authentication, framing and measurement choices constrain interpretation of these results? We examine one ESP32 platform and one archived campaign. The analysis retains every recorded observation, identifies the measurement intervals in the source, and distinguishes observations from symbolic and host-level checks. It is a descriptive implementation study; it does not test a causal performance advantage over TLS or establish a new cryptographic construction.

\subsection{Engineering objective and contributions}
The intended deployment consists of pre-provisioned nodes whose peers already share authentication credentials. The engineering objective is to characterize a certificate-free hybrid exchange integrated with Wi-Fi, an RTOS task and encrypted telemetry. Omitting certificate processing changes the authentication and provisioning model; a lower observed cost cannot automatically be attributed to a better cryptographic implementation.

The study makes three bounded contributions. First, it provides an inspectable integration of hybrid exchange, PSK transcript authentication and periodic telemetry-session renewal for a pre-provisioned ESP32 deployment. Second, it reports an operation-level trace that identifies the selected X25519 implementation as the principal local optimization target. Third, it reanalyses an archived application campaign while distinguishing it from separate primitive, TLS and heap acquisitions. In the primitive trace, the two measured X25519 operation means sum to 592.80~ms, about 18.3 times the 32.47~ms sum of the two measured client-side ML-KEM-768 operations. This trace-specific observation is not a matched end-to-end comparison and does not show that X25519 is inherently slower.

\subsection{Handshake encoding and key derivation}
The client sends one binary request to \texttt{/handshake}. Table~\ref{tab:messages} gives the hybrid body fields. HTTP headers and TCP/IP overhead are excluded from the listed lengths. The application exchange has one request and one response after transport establishment. The campaign creates and cleans up an HTTP client per handshake; persistent connection reuse is not established.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Hybrid application-body encoding, in transmission order.}
\label{tab:messages}

\midrule

Response & Session ID (16), server X25519 public key (32), KEM ciphertext (1,088), HMAC (32) & 1,168\\

HMAC-SHA256~\cite{b24} authenticates the request's first 1,217 bytes. The response HMAC covers those same unsigned request bytes followed by the response's Session ID, server public key and KEM ciphertext. It excludes both authentication tags. Verification therefore binds the server response to the current client parameters. The server authenticates possession of the PSK in the request; confirmation that the client derived the same traffic key arrives through valid telemetry, without a separate client-finished message.

The shared input is the 32-byte X25519 secret followed by the 32-byte KEM secret. HKDF-SHA256~\cite{b20,b33} produces 32 bytes using salt \texttt{HybridPQC-ESP32-Session-v1} and info \texttt{session-key}. The PSK authenticates the transcript and is not input entropy to HKDF. The published evaluation PSK is public and offers no private-credential authentication to someone who can read the repository. A security experiment must provision private credentials separately.

Before concatenating the secrets, the firmware scans the X25519 output and rejects an all-zero value as required by RFC~7748~\cite{b19}. The host test suite checks the accumulator logic and server-side rejection of an all-zero input point. These checks are not a hardware integration test of the corrected firmware; the archived campaign predates the later harness corrections.

The server creates the 16-byte Session ID using Python's \texttt{secrets.token\_bytes(16)} and the operating-system CSPRNG. The client uses ESP-IDF randomness for its local operations; true-random output depends on the documented entropy-source conditions~\cite{b43}. A Session ID is an identifier, not a substitute for transcript authentication.

\subsection{Telemetry and task lifecycle}
Telemetry uses AES-256-GCM~\cite{b22,b29,b30,b35} without additional authenticated data. Its 12-byte IV contains a four-byte counter followed by eight random bytes. The server checks that received request counters advance. After 50 successfully processed telemetry responses, the client leaves the loop, cleans up the context and starts a fresh handshake. The archived rotation trace supports historical behavior, not execution of the later corrected firmware.

Both directions currently use the same traffic key. Counter checks do not bind a message to a sending role, so directional reflection and cross-direction replay remain limitations. The software is intended for protocol inspection and laboratory evaluation. It should not be used as a secure Internet deployment without redesign and validation of these boundaries.

\section{Reproduction methods and implementation checks}
The code is distributed under the repository's MIT license; dependency licenses remain applicable. The native host build uses a Linux/macOS-compatible C toolchain and Make. Python dependencies are listed in \texttt{server/requirements.txt}. ESP32 reproduction requires ESP-IDF v5.5, a suitable board and a reachable Wi-Fi/server configuration. Commands and metadata requirements are supplied in \texttt{docs/reproduction\_steps.md} and S2.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Check out the exact frozen source version. Recompute historical statistics using the check mode of \texttt{tools/analyze\_submission.py}, as documented in the reproduction guide. This compares numerical records with the UART trace and verifies the generated manuscript values.
\item Build the native library with \texttt{make -C firmware/components/mlkem768}. Install the declared Python requirements, then run \texttt{python3 -m unittest discover -s tests} and \texttt{python3 tools/test\_mlkem768\_kat.py}.
\item Start \texttt{python3 server/server.py} on its default port 8443. Configure local Wi-Fi and server settings in the ignored \texttt{wifi\_config.local.h}; keep private credentials out of published files.
\item In an ESP-IDF terminal, select target \texttt{esp32}, build, flash and capture the full serial output. Preserve the exact commit, toolchain, configuration, ELF/map and binary hashes. Retain all attempts and failures, rather than selecting only completed fast runs.
\end{enumerate}

The legacy \texttt{test\_mlkem768\_kat.py} filename is retained for compatibility. It checks fixed-seed and randomized round trips and modified ciphertext behavior. It does not compare authoritative external expected-answer vectors and does not establish NIST certification. Current Windows host checks exercise both server entry points' framing/HMAC behavior with a substituted KEM output, and missing-library failure behavior. These checks passed in the preparation environment; they are not native KEM or hardware measurements. Full native-library tests require a compatible toolchain and runtime.

The current harness preserves attempted-run identifiers and computes successful-sample statistics separately. It also removes invalid watchdog-reset calls from an unsubscribed task. Host regression checks address failure accounting, but do not validate scheduling or watchdog behavior on an ESP32. A new hardware campaign must be saved separately if these later corrections are to be called physically validated.

OpenAI Codex assisted with evidence-consistency checks, analysis scripting, source and documentation edits, manuscript editing and preparation of Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}. It generated no hardware measurements. The authors are responsible for independently reviewing and validating the final manuscript, all cited claims and all reported values before submission.

The sums of the two X25519 and two client-side ML-KEM operation means are 592.80 and 32.47~ms, respectively, a ratio of approximately 18.3:1 in this primitive trace. This identifies the selected X25519 implementation as the principal local optimization target. The sums use unequal sample counts, exclude server encapsulation and are not measured fractions of an individual complete handshake. They must not be subtracted from the separately acquired campaign mean to estimate network overhead or presented as an algorithmic comparison.

The archive also contains real, but small and separate, TLS observations. Two wolfSSL TLS~1.3 exchanges explicitly negotiate \texttt{X25519MLKEM768}, with observed durations 1092.763 and 1101.846~ms (mean 1097.30~ms, $n=2$). Four classical TLS observations span 798.797 to 2350.741~ms. A separate custom-protocol excerpt records 849.604~ms. These runs document that the experiments were performed; they are exploratory context only because sample counts, authentication, acquisition conditions and build records are not matched. They do not establish a controlled percentage speedup or peak-memory saving over TLS. Before/after free-heap snapshots do not measure transient peak allocation.

The confidence interval uses Student's $t$ distribution with 99 degrees of freedom and sample SD. It assumes independent observations within this campaign and does not quantify variability across devices or environments. The 100 completed records describe the archived acquisition window; they do not establish the later harness's all-attempt success rate. The archive contains 100 watchdog API ``task not found'' errors, disclosed separately from handshake completion. These log messages do not prove hardware watchdog resets.

A separate heap profile reports a peak dynamic allocation of 13.8~KB, excluding the configured task stack and static storage; the underlying transient trace is incomplete. This profile is independent of the $n=100$ campaign. Campaign snapshots average 22.24 bytes and reach 440 bytes, but miss transient allocations and are not a campaign peak. Primitive timings, manual electrical notes and TLS traces were collected separately. They do not form a controlled, authentication-equivalent efficiency comparison. Energy values derived from fixed windows are not synchronized power integrals.

Archived ProVerif output~\cite{b25} establishes encoded client/server payload confidentiality and client-to-server injective agreement under idealized operations and an uncompromised private PSK. It does not query later PSK disclosure, reverse authentication, concrete GCM nonces or physical memory exposure. Selected client-buffer zeroization is not evidence of complete server-side erasure or resistance to a live memory dump. Empirical side-channel resistance remains unevaluated.

The recorded firmware version is \texttt{v2.0-mlkem768-final-6-g45d5f57-}; its ELF digest is truncated, so an exact historical binary-to-source mapping is unavailable. Synthetic PCAP illustrations remain illustrative and are not wire captures. The recovered master tcpdump text is now included in the audit materials (SHA-256: \texttt{958cf70de291297887b70c49e2e6cb8c90e44fc6f5b48036c4da7758867c4494}); an independent parser reproduces the supplied count and port totals of 14,157 endpoint-flow records (10,235 on port 8443 and 3,922 on port 4443). The raw file records 17,502 client SYN events, but SYN events and parser-accepted TCP flows are not cryptographic attempts or successful handshakes. Port numbers alone do not identify TLS mode, device, or firmware, and the capture does not independently establish uninterrupted operation or a success rate. S1 preserves these limits and the separate acquisition boundaries.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
The testbed provides source-level access to the PSK-authenticated exchange, its telemetry lifecycle and its historical measurement analysis. Researchers can reuse its encodings and analysis interfaces to study implementation changes while preserving acquisition provenance. A new experiment should identify its own build, failures and raw observations, and use matched authentication and instrumentation when comparing protocol costs.

The primitive acquisition identifies the selected X25519 implementation, rather than client-side ML-KEM, as the dominant local cost in that trace (592.80~ms against 32.47~ms for the two measured ML-KEM operations). This provides a concrete optimization priority for this software configuration, although a matched before-and-after experiment is required to demonstrate an improvement. The historical campaign yields a mean instrumented latency of \BenchmarkMeanMs~ms and mean elapsed CCOUNT of \BenchmarkCyclesM~million cycles on the stated ESP32 setup. Together these records characterize one certificate-free, PSK-authenticated application-layer prototype; they do not show superiority to TLS. Direction-separated traffic keys, stronger implementation correctness checks and controlled memory/energy measurements are necessary for stronger security or efficiency claims. No production-readiness, first-implementation or new-primitive claim is made.
